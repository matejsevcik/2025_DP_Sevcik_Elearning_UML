\section{Solution description}

When designing our intended class diagram courses, we will take into account a number of requirements. Firstly, we have the platform or \ac{lms} on which to implement the course, then there are e-learning techniques to use, specific types of exercises and other educational content we want to apply, and lastly we need to propose an effective and objective evaluation method to determine the potential of the project for further research. 

Additional constraint that we need to account for is the scope of the course. Even though an ideal \ac{uml} course might guide the user through building an entire system, this course is complementary and the software modeling class's curriculum does not make space for it. Participation in this course is voluntary. For these reasons, we decided to restrict the course's scope to only one diagram type to test out the concept, and to fit all the content into one hour. 
% A longer course would likely discourage participation from the students.

The \ac{uml} class diagram was preferred over other diagram types, because it is one of the most prominent \ac{uml} diagram types - likely the most widely used one - and it is intuitive to understand. It directly links to coding which students tend to comprehend better than abstract diagrams \cite{umple}.

\subsection{E-learning platform}

We want to create the courses on a preexisting \ac{lms}. Doing so will significantly reduce development time, allowing us to focus on the educational content. 

The platform should satisfy the following conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item easy to use and learn
    \item record and store students' scores and progress for result comparison and material recommendations 
    \item option to propose changes and improvements, report mistakes and bugs
    \item easy to include new features and content 
    \item free to use or open source
    \item support for e-learning techniques described in the next sections % gamification, microlearning, social learning, adaptive learning
    \item support for interactive exercises
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Content Structure}

This section is aimed at describing what specific types of content the course should contain. We will divide it into four sections - an introductory test to assess the starting point of the students, a theoretical section dedicated to explaining concepts, one for practical exercises, and one for final skill assessment after taking the course. The final test will be accompanied by a questionnaire.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Introductory assessment test
    \item Theory
    \item Practical exercises
    \item Final assessment test, questionnaire
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Activity diagram - student}

The activity diagram describes a student's interaction with the system. The student will log in to the system and view a course. They will complete the introductory test. Then they will proceed to the theoretical section with micro-exercises in between modules and the practical section containing more exercises. Content will be recommended based on how successful their responses were across various topics. The student can come back to the theoretical section and the exercises and redo them until they are satisfied with their results. Lastly, the student will enter the evaluation section, where they will complete a test (which will not give them instant feedback this time), and fill in a feedback questionnaire.

\subsection{Content - Exercises} 
% The student will be given material recommendations based on the mistakes they made in the exercises. 

A limitation of the exercise design is the absence of a \ac{uml} diagram editor. We do not have the capacity to employ teachers to manually assess modeling exercises. At the same time, designing a comprehensive automated diagram assessment tool is a non-trivial problem and is beyond the scope of this project.

For these reasons, we will replace direct modeling tasks with a set of exercises implemented using more general, readily available formats. The exercise design is inspired by findings from our analysis, namely:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Searching for mistakes in others' diagrams can enhance the students' modeling skills \cite{shmallo2020constructive}
    \item Students develop a better understanding of \ac{uml} when they see diagrams translated to code \cite{umple}
    \item Exercises work best when feedback is immediate and explains why the answer is correct or incorrect \cite{al2022effect}
\end{itemize}

They will be of the following types:

\begin{itemize} \label{exercises_design}
    \item Fill in missing parts of a diagram (relationships, multiplicity...). This is the most important exercise type, since it directly simulates modeling
    \item Assign the type of relationship to a description or a code snippet
    \item Find mistakes or improvements in a diagram
    \item Decide, which of the diagrams contains a mistake
    \item Compare a diagram with a code snippet
    \item Alter a diagram based on instructions
    \item Match class diagram components with their descriptions and pictures
    \item Theoretical questions
\end{itemize}

The sections with tests will consist of a number of exercises selected from across all the course's topics. The results will be recorded and analyzed later.

\subsection{E-learning techniques}

Based on our analysis, we concluded that the most suitable e-learning techniques are microlearning, gamification, adaptive learning and social learning. Our courses will also apply self-directed learning, since a course can be taken at any time and at any place, and blended learning, as it is meant to be a complementary tool to normal classes rather than a stand-alone learning material. 

Under microlearning we understand breaking down content in the sections dedicated to explaining the concepts into small pieces.

Gamification offers the largest amount of possible features in an online course out of all the e-learning techniques. 

It is important to note that there is a limited number of gamified elements that can be implemented. One of the main constraints on them is that they must not overwhelm or confuse the students. The one-hour format of the courses does not provide the space to use more complicated mechanics associated with gamification, as they might take time to get used to. It was observed that some gamified courses slowed the learners down first before increasing their performance due to the need to adapt to the different format of the study method \cite{kim2015dynamical}.

Examples of such techniques ae levels, experience points, purchasable items within the course or meaningful quests, which are common instances of gamification. Instead, we want to use storytelling and role-play, which might be even more efficient than artificial awards such as points \cite{gamification_personalization}. We will use the role-play as the main distinction between the 2 versions of the courses, giving the narrator of the instructions and theoretical sections a different personality - one will be fun and warm, the other brief, demanding and professional. We want to further enhance the mood of the courses by selecting appropriate - but applicable - e-learning techniques.

The 2 moods for the course were chosen because competition and fun are among the most commonly used gamification elements. Fun is associated with rewards, humor, emotional ease, and playfulness, while competition aims at self-accomplishment and seeking achievements. That makes the fun, playful design and the competitive design ideal for comparison.

The competitive version of the course will contain a leaderboard and display metrics of other students (more on that later in the same section). We decided not to use a timer for tasks even though it would fit the mood of the course, since it presents hindrance for students with lower base knowledge and encourages rushing through materials.

The playful version of the course will strive to increase the students' emotional engagement by providing visual rewards for correct answers. Another element to compliment the design are badges awarded for reaching milestones within the course, which are a popular addition to gamified environments - despite not being sufficient to engage students on their own. Lastly, students will be given funny nicknames to attract their attention at the start of the course.

On top of that, we plan to implement visual changes triggered by progress in the courses, mistakes or difficulty level of exercises. 
% We plan to implement a system for awarding experience points for completing tasks, where reaching certain milestones will trigger some changes in the aesthetics of the course. They will be used for tracking and visualizing progress in the course and exercises will be ranked by difficulty - the more difficult exercises will award more points. Another gamification element we want to include is visually enhanced feedback.

Adaptive learning should allow the course to propose content to the student based on their mistakes in exercises. Feedback should be specific and explain why an answer is incorrect.

When it comes to social learning, collaborations on modeling tasks are not allowed by the experiment format - students will take the courses on their own outside of classes. For this reason, we will restrict the practice to displaying a number of metrics to the users, such as the average score on a given exercise, average completion time or the overall progress of other students in the course. Doing so will apply some social pressure on the students to perform better and potentially trigger their competitive tendencies. This practice is sometimes mentioned as a part of gamification. 

Since such practices are sometimes questioned in literature, we will restrict this functionality to the competitive version of the course.

E-learning techniques that are to be implemented:

\begin{itemize}
    \item microlearning
    \item gamification (narration, badges...)
    \item adaptive learning (feedback, content recommendations)
    \item social learning (performance comparisons, leaderboards - restricted to competitive course)
    \item self-paced learning
    \item blended learning
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation}

Tested students will be divided into 2 groups - each student will only take one of the course versions. That will allow for separate analysis of effects of the different course designs on the students.

The first part of evaluation will consist of a questionnaire with questions about subjective opinions and impressions of the students. The most important questions will concern whether the students thought the course was helpful, whether it improved their understanding of the class diagram and, most importantly, how the e-learning elements impacted their perceptions and behaviors during and after taking the course.

The second, objective part of the evaluation will consist of 2 tests containing theoretical questions and practical exercises about the class diagram. One of the tests will be taken before completing the course, the other one after. They will be used to evaluate the progress of the students. The same tests will be taken by both groups.

The last part of the evaluation will measure the students' behavior - such as time taken to complete activities and the amount of interactions with the system's elements under each course design.
