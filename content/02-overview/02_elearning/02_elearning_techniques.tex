\section{Impacts of various e-learning techniques on students}

There are numerous techniques applied in e-learning materials. Some of them are aimed at driving engagement by introducing game-like aspects to educational content (gamification), some help structure materials better by dividing into small, manageable pieces (microlearning) or by making personalized content recommendations to students (adaptive learning). Social learning strives to leverage positive effects of group activities despite the digital setting.

These techniques can be implemented in numerous ways, and they do not have the same effect on the learners. Using too many is likely to overwhelm the students and worsen their learning experience, so it is important to choose only a limited number of them.

A study \cite{al2022effect} suggests the type of feedback delivered after each exercise can significantly impact the students' motivation. Apart from being immediate, feedback can take various forms. The study reveals the most potent form is providing not only a confirmation of the result (correct/incorrect), but also explaining why the answer is correct or incorrect, increasing both the students' motivation and objective performance in tests more significantly than other feedback approaches.

Another study \cite{gamification_personalization} compares the effects of gamification and personalization in e-learning environments. By gamification, it means immersion practices such as avatars, profiles and storytelling/role-play, social features (teams, cooperation, competition) or the more achievement based techniques such as leaderboards, challenges, points and rankings. 

Personalization means tailoring content to the students' individual needs by providing self-paced environment, personalized feedback from instructors or personalized content selection or even adaptive learning paths. This can be described as adaptive learning. The recommendations can be based on user data like their major or fields of interest, their behavior or in the types of mistakes they make, among other things.

The key findings of the study are that while personalization drives cognitive learning engagement, gamification promotes emotional learning engagement. Though not surprising, the implications are personalization improves learning efficiency, while gamification can be applied to keep the students engaged and persist in the process. Gamification is then more of a psychological than objective factor. 

Another important finding is that different studied gamification elements have varying effect. The social elements (collaboration, competition) and immersion elements (avatars, storytelling) were of higher importance than achievement elements (experience points, badges). It does state the achievement elements are useful nonetheless.

Though the reception of gamification is positive in almost all research papers we reviewed, some works suggest it is not so efficient in all instances\cite{haters}. However, the study aggregates many research papers across various fields, target audiences and inconsistent gamification implementations. Despite potential issues with the study, it shows that gamification is not automatically an improvement to study materials and its effectiveness depends on context and its implementation. 

This point is further supported by Luo et al.\cite{luo2022gamification}. It sums up other research in which gamification was not implemented successfully. The failures are linked to shift from intrinsic motivation (interest in learning) towards earning points and rank. Other potential issue was poor implementation of the gamification elements, making the students more confused than motivated.

Luo et al.\cite{luo2022gamification} mentions that among the most significant barriers to gamification implementation were \enquote{Inappropriate or superficial use of gamification techniques} and \enquote{Poor integration of game features and pedagogical content}.

Humor too as a part of e-learning materials has the potential to enhance the students' motivation and learning outcomes \cite{chin2010learning}.
